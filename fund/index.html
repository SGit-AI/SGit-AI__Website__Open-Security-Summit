Sponsor

The capability is here. It needs funding.

For years the summit was kept alive by one person paying for it, at a cost, without the people to follow up on what it produced. Today the follow-up can be done. What is missing is the money to run the events.

The first sponsors

  • Sponsor

    RiskMandate

    Give agents access, not free rein: Agent Behaviour Policies that write down what an AI agent can really reach, what it was authorised to do, and the gap between the two. A founding contributor to the first hosted project.

  • Sponsor

    The Cyber Boardroom

    The one-stop shop boards of directors rely on for their cyber readiness. It runs RiskMandate today.

  • Sponsor

    SGit.ai

    The encrypted git for humans and AI agents: vaults versioned like git, handed over with a single read key. This site and the evidence behind the summit's sessions are built on it.

Disclosure: Dinis Cruz, who directs Open Security Summit CIC, is involved in all three. That is why they are first, and it is why the rule below matters: they get no more say in the sessions than anyone else. More sponsors, from outside, are what the summit needs next.

What it pays for

  • The working sessions. Running, recording and following up each one. See the next phase.
  • The London events. A venue, the days, the recording, and travel for the people who could not otherwise come.
  • The archive. Reading eight years of sessions and recordings into pages people can use.
  • The hosted projects. The legal and administrative work of hosting them properly.

The rule

Sponsors do not buy sessions, speaking slots or content. That was true at every in-person summit, and it is what made the room trustworthy. A sponsor is named and thanked, and its people take part on the same terms as everyone else.

Sponsor a session or a series

The cheapest way to start is one session, or a series on one subject. A sponsor of a session is named on its page and in its follow-up, and does not choose who speaks or what the outcome says.
