The next phase: Open Security Summit sessions called when the participants can make them, not a week-long event, starting with the php2shell PHP flaws

The next phase of the Open Security Summit is not a big week-long event. It is a regular run of short working sessions, each called when the people who need to be in it can make it, on subjects that matter now, each with a page, an outcome and a follow-up. The aim is to start generating energy and sponsors again, and the bigger London events come out of that. The first session will be on php2shell, the two critical PHP flaws of October 2026, which the SGit newsroom is covering as evidence.

In short

  • Sessions, not a week. A regular run of short working sessions, not one big event a year.
  • Scheduled around the participants. A session is called when the people who need to be in it can make it, not slotted into a fixed grid.
  • On what matters now. Incidents, decisions and projects, while they are live.
  • Each one finished. A page before, a recording, an outcome, and a follow-up after.
  • The aim is energy and sponsors. A steady stream of good sessions is what brings people back, and what a sponsor can see and support.
  • The first is php2shell. The two critical PHP flaws of October 2026, with the evidence the newsroom has gathered.

Why not a week-long event

The summit weeks at Woburn were the best of what we did, and they were also the hardest thing to start again. A week needs a venue, months of planning, a full schedule, the money up front, and everyone free on the same five days. The online mini-summits copied that shape: a week of sessions every month or two, fitted into a grid. When the energy dropped, the whole shape stopped with it.

What made those weeks work was never the week. It was the working session: a topic, an owner, the right people in the room, and something written at the end. That unit can stand on its own. It does not need a week around it.

How it works

  • A session starts with a subject and an owner. Someone who wants to work on something with others: an incident, a draft, a decision, a project.
  • It is called when the people can make it. The owner names who needs to be in it; the date follows their diaries, inside a week or two, rather than waiting for the next event.
  • It is short. An hour, sometimes two, online. Recorded unless the participants say otherwise.
  • It has a page before it happens. The subject, the questions, the material to read, who is in it, and how to join.
  • It ends with an outcome, and a follow-up. What was decided or written, sent to the people who took part, with the open items kept visible. This is the part the summit never had the people for, and the part agents can now do, under the owner's responsibility.
  • It is regular. Not a fixed calendar, but a steady rhythm, so that there is always a next session.

Every session is a working session, and every participant is a presenter. That rule does not change.

Why this generates energy, and sponsors

A week is one bet a year. A run of sessions is many small ones, and each one leaves something behind: a page, a recording, an outcome, and people who want to come to the next one. Subjects that are live, like an incident in its first weeks, bring in people who would never book a week. The ones who keep coming become the owners of the next sessions.

It is also something a sponsor can see. A sponsor can support one session, a series on one subject, or the programme, and point at what it produced. The first sponsors are RiskMandate, The Cyber Boardroom and SGit.ai; the rule is the one we always had, that sponsorship buys no content. As the sessions and the sponsors build, the in-person London events come out of them, rather than having to come first.

The first session: php2shell

On 21 October 2026 the PHP project released fixes for two critical flaws in the PHP engine, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, nicknamed php2shell. Either can give an unauthenticated attacker remote code execution on a web server running PHP. The flaws were found with AI help, and three parties claim the discovery. The coordinated disclosure broke down a day early. And by the next morning the NSA, CISA and the UK's NCSC were telling organisations that could not patch to take systems offline, while other national agencies gave no such advice.

The SGit newsroom is covering it as evidence at php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai: every claim with a status and a captured, hashed source, a timeline of the events, the agencies side by side, and a briefing for each kind of reader. That is the material the session starts from.

It is a good first session because it has everything the summit is about. Developers and security people both have a stake in it. The open questions are real and unresolved. And what the people in the room know is worth more than any single write-up. The questions it will work on:

  • The disclosure. What broke on 20 October, and what a coordinated disclosure for a runtime this widely used should look like.
  • AI-assisted discovery. Two old engine bugs, one first seen in a 2024 audit, found with models by three parties at once. What the models did, what the people did, and what that means for the next one.
  • Offline or not. Why the agencies disagreed on taking systems offline, and what a CISO should have done with that advice.
  • The long tail. PHP 8.1 and older are out of support, with source backports nobody upstream builds. Who is left holding those servers.
  • Evidence over claims. No named organisation has confirmed a compromise, while attempts are counted in the millions. How to report an incident without saying more than the evidence does.

The session page, with how to join, is here. The date will follow the diaries of the people who need to be in it.

What this does not claim

  • No date is set. For the php2shell session or the ones after it.
  • The follow-up by agents is not running yet. It is the first thing this site builds for the sessions.
  • The php2shell facts are the newsroom's, as of 23 October. They are developing; the newsroom site has the current status of each claim.
  • The sponsors are connected to the organiser. Dinis Cruz is involved in all three, as the fund page says.
